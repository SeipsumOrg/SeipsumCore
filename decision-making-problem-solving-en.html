Decision Making & Problem Solving — Definitions | Mechanics | Dynamics


A decision is not an event. A problem is not a situation. Both are structural states that thinking either reaches or does not.


Observed over time, decisions and problems do not fail at the moment of choosing or solving — they fail earlier, in the structure of the field the choice or solution is made in: overlapping variables, undifferentiated levels, unclarified values.


The objective here is not advice or simplification, but the mechanics of how decisions and problems form, why they destabilise, and what structure does to clarity under uncertainty.

The Main Observation


The quality of decisions and problem solving is not determined at the moment of choosing or solving, but by the structure of the variables, levels, and values present in cognition before the attempt is made.

What a Decision Is


A decision is the point where a set of cognitive variables resolves toward action.


Before that point, a situation exists as an unstable field: relevant and irrelevant elements overlap, interactions remain undefined, and contradictory values remain unclarified. The decision is the moment this field compresses into a direction.


This is why decisions are not primarily about information. They are about the structure of the information already present.

What a Problem Is


A problem exists as a representation before it exists as a situation.


No situation is encountered directly, alone. Because each situation is encountered through a representation — an internal compression of what the situation is, where it begins, what it contains, and what it excludes.


The representation is not a summary of the situation. It is a structure: it determines which variables are visible, which are excluded, which interactions appear to exist, and which levels of the situation are treated as the problem itself.


This is why the same situation is a different problem for different cognitive structures.


What appears as a problem of resources to one structure appears as a problem of sequencing to another, and a problem of conflicting values to a third. The situation is identical. The representation differs. And the resolution differs accordingly — because the problem being solved is never the situation. It is the representation.

Problems, Structurally


A problem exists as a representation before it exists as a situation.


  • Every situation is encountered through a representation — an internal compression of what the situation is. The representation determines which variables are visible, which are excluded, and which interactions appear to exist.

  • Problem statements compress. Compression excludes variables — some correctly, some silently. A representation that excludes a load-bearing variable produces a problem that cannot be stably solved, because the thing that matters is not in the formulation.

  • The same situation is a different problem for different cognitive structures. What appears as a problem of resources to one structure appears as a problem of sequencing to another, and a problem of conflicting values to a third. The situation is identical; the representation differs; the resolution differs accordingly.

  • Premature problem-definition — compressing a complex situation into a simplified narrative or fixed position before its variables are separated — produces decisions that are stable only until the excluded variables resurface.

Solving, structurally, is not the addition of new information. It is the reorganisation of the existing representation.


This is why more analysis can destabilise decisions: additional input applied to an unstructured representation does not resolve the field — it enlarges it. More information can produce less clarity. The instability was never caused by the absence of information.


The Mechanics of Decision Instability


Unstable or delayed decisions are rarely a sign of insufficient intelligence or insufficient data.


They are a sign of unresolved structure.

The mechanics, as observed:

  • Overlapping variables. When relevant and irrelevant elements are not separated, every option appears to carry the same weight. Everything seems equally important. The decision cannot resolve because there is nothing stable to resolve between.

  • Undifferentiated levels. Thinking operates on interdependent levels — surface events, structural patterns, and deep causal factors. Without structure, these levels overlap and generate confusion, decision instability, and perceptual fragmentation. A decision attempted across mixed levels inherits the instability of all of them.

  • Unclarified value conflicts. Contradictory values that remain undefined produce oscillation: each option appears correct from one angle and incorrect from another. The oscillation is not weakness of character. It is two unresolved variables competing for the same resolution point.

  • abstraction transfer to different domains.

  • Cognitive noise. Unstructured complexity becomes noise. When noise dominates, the load-bearing elements of a situation — the few variables on which the outcome actually depends — remain unidentified. The decision wobbles because it is standing on unidentified supports.

The problem is not the thinking. The problem is the lack of structure in thinking.

The Mechanics of Problem Instability


The same mechanics operate one step earlier — at the point where problems are formed rather than solved.


  • Compression excludes. Every problem statement is a compression, and compression excludes variables — some correctly, some silently. A representation that excludes a load-bearing variable produces a problem that cannot be stably solved, because the thing that matters is not in the formulation. The effort of solving is then spent on a structure that does not hold the outcome.

  • Premature definition. Compressing a complex situation into a simplified narrative or fixed position before its variables are separated produces problems that appear stable — and remain stable only until the excluded variables resurface. What feels like a solved problem returning is usually an excluded variable re-entering the representation.

  • Hidden assumptions. A problem representation carries assumptions about what kind of problem it is — and therefore about what kind of solution applies. These assumptions are rarely visible from inside the representation. Two contradictory attempts to solve "the same" problem often indicate two different hidden formulations.

  • Level mixing. When surface events, structural patterns, and causal factors are compressed into a single problem statement, attempts to solve it act on mixed levels at once — which is why identical efforts produce inconsistent results: each attempt resolves a different level, and the unresolved levels regenerate the problem.

  • Circularity. When a problem's formulation contains the supposed solution's failure, the loop closes: the representation regenerates the problem it was meant to resolve. Circular problems are rarely situations that resist effort. They are representations that produce the effort and absorb it.

The problem is not the difficulty of the problem. It is the structure of the representation that holds it.

The Bridge: From Problem to Decision


Solving, structurally, is not the addition of new information. It is the reorganisation of the existing representation.


Problems and decisions are not two subjects. They are the same mechanics at two stages:

  • A problem is an unresolved representation — a field in which the relevant variables are unidentified, mixed, or silently excluded.

  • Solving is the reorganisation of that representation: separating the relevant from the irrelevant, differentiating the levels, making the load-bearing variables visible.

  • A decision is the resolution that a reorganised field makes possible — the point where the clarified variables compress toward action.

This is why solving and deciding fail together. An unstable problem representation cannot produce a stable decision, because the decision inherits every unresolved element of the formulation. And a stable decision cannot be manufactured by choosing more carefully — the instability lives upstream of the choice.


What stabilises the decision is what stabilised the problem: structure, not effort.

Analysis, Overthinking, and Their Inversion


Two observations from practice:


  • More analysis can destabilise both. Analysis applied without variable separation multiplies the elements in play. The field grows faster than it differentiates. What was a problem becomes an investigation; what was a decision becomes a research project. More information can produce less clarity — the instability was not caused by the absence of information.

  • Overthinking is not a dysfunction in itself. Excessive thinking appears when perceived or real complexity exceeds the structuring capacity of cognition. Within structured cognition, the same activity becomes an advanced cognitive instrument — depth applied to a field that can hold it.

The difference between a dysfunction and an instrument is not the activity. It is the structure beneath the activity.

Decisions and Problems Under Uncertainty


Uncertainty does not remove the possibility of stable decisions. It changes what stability means.


  • Under certainty, a decision is stable when its representation matches the situation.

  • Under uncertainty, a decision is stable when the load-bearing variables are identified, the values in tension are clarified, and the point of resolution is explicit — regardless of what remains unknown.

  • What destabilises decisions under uncertainty is not the unknown. It is the unresolved: overlapping variables, unclarified conflicts, unidentified dependencies. These remain destabilising even in situations that are otherwise fully known.

The same holds for problems: a situation at no point fully known can still be represented stably — its variables separated, its levels differentiated, its exclusions visible. The formulation does not need to be complete. It needs to be structured.


Stability — in problems and in decisions — is produced by structure, not by the reduction of uncertainty.

What This Means


A decision does not fail at the moment of choosing. It fails earlier — in the structure of the field the choice is made in.


A problem does not resist because it is difficult. It resists because of how it is held — in a representation that has not been reorganised.


Clarity, in both, does not come from addition. It comes through reorganisation: separating signal from noise, differentiating the levels of a problem, clarifying the interactions, variables, and dynamics, and clarifying contradictory values.


The result is not an answer. It is a stable place from which to solve, and a stable place from which to decide.
