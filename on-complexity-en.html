On Complexity — Definitions | Mechanics | Dynamics


Complexity is not the amount of information a situation contains. It is the state of that information in cognition: undifferentiated, unseparated, held as one mass. A situation is complex to the cognition that has not differentiated it — and the same situation is not complex to a cognition that has.


This is why complexity is not reduced by removing elements. It is reduced by separating them. And this is why the perception of complexity and the structure of complexity are two different things — often with two different remedies.


The objective here is not advice or simplification, but the mechanics of what makes something complex in cognition, why undifferentiated complexity destabilises, and what differentiation does to it.

The Main Observation


Complexity is not a property of situations alone. It is a relation between a situation and the structure of the cognition that holds it: the same situation is complex to an undifferentiated field and tractable to a differentiated one. Complexity succeeds or fails in the intangible layer — what has been separated, what levels are distinct — and surfaces as tangible experience: what feels overwhelming, what feels manageable, what is acted on.

What Complexity Is


Complexity is undifferentiated informational mass: elements held together without separation — variables, levels, interactions, dependencies — where none is marked as load-bearing and none as irrelevant.


The amount is not the differentiator. A large differentiated field is not complex in this sense — it is large and clear. A small undifferentiated one is complex — it is small and opaque. What makes complexity is not size; it is the absence of separation.

What Complexity Is Not


Complexity is not difficulty. A situation can be difficult and fully differentiated — one hard, visible problem, load-bearing variables identified. Difficulty is a property of the load-bearing variables; complexity is a property of the field that holds them.


Complexity is not volume. More information, separated, does not make a situation more complex — it makes it larger. Volume becomes complexity only when it enters a field that cannot differentiate it.

Real Complexity and Perceived Complexity


Two observations from practice:


  • Real complexity is structural. It consists of the actual interactions, dependencies, and levels present in the situation — what must be differentiated before the situation can be operated on. Real complexity cannot be reduced; it can only be mapped.

  • Perceived complexity is cognitive. It is the experience of the undifferentiated field: everything appearing equally weighty, nothing safely excludable, attention saturating. Perceived complexity can be reduced — by separation, without the situation changing at all.

The two can exist together, and one can exist without the other. A situation of high real complexity, well differentiated, can feel manageable; a situation of low real complexity, undifferentiated, can feel overwhelming. Most of what is experienced as "too complex" is a report about the field, not about the situation.

Useful Complexity and Useless Complexity


Not all complexity is to be removed. Two observations:


  • Useful complexity is load-bearing. Some interactions and dependencies carry the outcome: removing them from the representation removes what the outcome stands on. Useful complexity is kept — visible, differentiated, worked with.

  • Useless complexity is generated. It is added by the field itself: duplicated levels, mixed categories, unresolved contradictions (for coherence), noise held as signal. Useless complexity is not in the situation — it is in the representation. It can be released.

The failure modes are symmetric: removing useful complexity collapses the representation; keeping useless complexity saturates it. Neither is corrected by effort — both are corrected by separation.

The Mechanics of Undifferentiated Complexity


A field that holds complexity undifferentiated follows mechanics, not accidents:

  • Equal apparent weight. Without separation, every element presents as equally important. Attention cannot rest, because there is no structural ground for preference.

  • Interaction opacity. What interacts with what — and through what — stays invisible. Effects are observed without their dependencies, and the situation appears to behave arbitrarily.

  • Level mixing. Events, patterns, and values held at one level cannot be distinguished. The field oscillates between treating a symptom as a cause and a value as a fact.

  • Compression by narrative. A predefined representation compresses the complexity in advance — some of it correctly, some silently. What the narrative removed is not known to be removed: the field feels simpler while having become less accurate.

The result is not a hard situation. It is an unseparated one — experienced as "complex."

The Mechanics of Differentiation


Differentiation of complexity follows mechanics, not discipline:

  • Levels first. Events, patterns, and values are separated before anything else: what happens, what repeats, what is at stake. Most perceived complexity dissolves at this step alone.

  • Load-bearing identification. The few variables on which the outcome actually depends are identified; everything else is marked as candidate noise — excluded visibly, reversibly.

  • Dependency mapping. Interactions are made explicit: what depends on what, through what, with what weight. Real complexity is not reduced here — it is mapped, which is the only operation it accepts.

  • Accretion, not accumulation. A differentiated field can grow large without becoming complex — each new element enters as separated, placed at its level, weighed against the load-bearing few. Accumulation without differentiation produces complexity; accretion with differentiation produces structure.

The Bridge: From Complexity to Compression


  • Differentiation and compression are not two operations. They are the same mechanics at two stages: what a field separates, compression can then act on — and what compression removes was either separated or lost.

  • Smarter compression — not accumulation — is what differentiated processing enables: fewer elements held, but each load-bearing. The most capable cognition is not the one holding the most; it is the one that needs the least to hold the outcome.

    • What reduces experienced complexity is what built the field: separation, not removal.

Complexity Under Uncertainty


Two observations from practice:

  • Uncertainty does not remove the possibility of differentiation. It changes what differentiation operates on: under uncertainty, the levels and the load-bearing few are identified from incomplete data — differentiated, but provisionally, with visibility on what is assumed.

  • What destabilises differentiation under uncertainty is not the unknown. It is the unresolved (for coherence): unidentified dependencies, unclarified values, mixed levels. These generate complexity of their own — a complexity that belongs to the field, layered over whatever the situation contains.

Experienced complexity under uncertainty is produced by the unresolved, not by the incomplete.

What This Means


Complexity is not the situation. It is the relation between the situation and the structure that holds it — which is why the same situation is complex to one cognition and not to another.


  • Real complexity is mapped, not reduced; perceived complexity is reduced by separation, not by effort. Useful complexity is load-bearing and kept; useless complexity is generated by the field and released. The failure modes are symmetric, and both are corrected by separation.

  • Clarity does not come from less. It comes through differentiation: levels separated, dependencies mapped, the load-bearing few identified, exclusions kept visible.

  • The work is intangible — separation, levels, mapping, visibility. What it stabilises is tangible: situations that hold, complexity that does not saturate, capacity that survives scale.

The result is not a simpler world. It is a differentiated representation of a complex one — and a field that can hold it.
